# Design direction

Adapted and modified by Tom Dale from Anthropic's frontend-design skill. Attribution and modification notice: NOTICE.md; license: LICENSE.txt.

## Set the freedom to design

Read the brief, real content, available assets, existing product, and applicable design-system guidance. Identify the audience, task, surface, and what is fixed versus open: identity, palette, typography, layout, interactions, and scope. A supplied direction governs its specified dimensions. A local change inherits the surrounding identity; express it precisely rather than turning it into a rebrand.

For a surface people operate, put expression into information design, useful emphasis, typography, and precise behavior while keeping familiar controls. For reading, make structure and rhythm carry the content. For persuasion or an expressive experience, the composition and imagery can take greater aesthetic risks. Choose by the surface's job, not the product category.

Reuse the reference sheet and decision record from design-references. For substantial work with unresolved research questions, use that skill, then continue here. Its target is four or more closely matched inspected examples where useful and available. Keep the shared record in task storage. Ask only about consequential unknowns; follow the session's approval rules.

## Find the subject-specific idea

Identify concrete material from this brief: what the product actually does, its output, content relationships, audience practices, place, or available imagery. Use those specifics to write one sentence connecting an idea to a visible or interactive decision. "Clean and modern" names a mood; the idea must explain what gets composed differently and why.

For example, a repair workshop might organize its page around actual objects and repair stages rather than interchangeable service cards. A review tool might make the relationship between explanation and changed code the organizing structure. These are starting hypotheses, not mandatory visual metaphors. Use real content and assets; identify illustrative material and preserve factual product claims.

## Explore composition before code

Where the direction is open, sketch two or three materially different approaches using the same representative content. Vary hierarchy, reading order, scale, density, imagery, or the relationship between controls and content; font and color swaps on one skeleton are not different approaches. Use compact visual studies when tools allow, rather than building several complete pages. When the brief already specifies a composition or the task is a small refinement, work directly within it.

Choose the approach that best expresses the subject and serves the task; state its trade-off. Seek a user choice when the workflow requires it or the alternatives represent consequential unresolved preferences. Otherwise recommend and proceed within scope. Carry the choice into the existing decision record: concept, first-view composition, type roles and scale, palette roles, spacing rhythm, available imagery, and intended behavior. Specify enough to build consistently; reuse established tokens where applicable.

## Challenge the default

Before building, ask whether the composition would be equally plausible after replacing the subject and content with an unrelated brief. Identify which decisions the actual brief changed. If only the logo, nouns, and accent changed, revisit the open dimensions with a different composition. Familiar navigation or a standard data table can remain the correct solution; distinctiveness belongs where the task and brief give it a job.

Inspect habitual choices such as an icon-card grid, a cream-and-serif editorial treatment, a neon-on-black technical theme, generic gradient decoration, or decorative metadata separators. Treat them as prompts for a decision, not automatic defects. Keep a familiar treatment when the brief, host, brand, content, or interaction earns it; explain that reason. Replacing one fashionable look with its fashionable opposite does not establish a concept.

## Build the chosen character

Make the defining move legible, then give supporting elements clear roles. Establish hierarchy through type scale, weight, measure, alignment, color roles, and spacing. Let containers and labels express real relationships. Use the subject's content, imagery, or mechanism as visual material where appropriate; decoration should support that idea. Match the voice to the audience and name actions by their real effects.

Carry the direction through supporting sections, controls, empty/error states, and narrow layouts. A distinctive opening followed by stock sections is incomplete execution. A working interface may need consistency and density; an expressive page may need abundant imagery or bold color. Set those choices from the brief rather than a fixed quota for fonts, images, whitespace, or animation. Use the installed motion skills when time-based behavior is part of the direction.

## Verify character and quality together

Run design-preflight on the whole rendered artifact against the brief, baseline where available, and chosen direction. Check both whether the result works and whether it actually delivers the intended composition and character. Preserve expressive decisions while correcting evidenced usability, accessibility, semantic, or functional problems. If the render is tidy but still interchangeable on the dimensions meant to distinguish it, revise the composition within scope rather than adding decorative accents.

Report the chosen idea, which brief details shaped it, meaningful conventions retained, and verification limits. Distinguish intentionality supported by the design record from measured reader outcomes or proof of originality.
